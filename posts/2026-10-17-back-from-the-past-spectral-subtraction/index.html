Back from the past: Audio denoising demo

October 17, 2026 · edited November 6, 2026 · 6 min · Benoit Fauve

I recently dusted off one of my first projects in speech processing: a demo with a simple graphical interface showing real-time denoising of an audio signal.

This project was part of an Erasmus break in the “Speech and Image Group” at the University of Wales Swansea, when I was finishing my degree at ENSPM (now Centrale Marseille).

Some fond memories, ticking the boxes and beyond#

I have some good memories of this project.

  • It marked my shift to speech processing, a subject that has kept me quite busy since. John Mason, who led the Speech and Image Group, would become my PhD supervisor a few years later.
  • It fitted my interest in research, while keeping the focus on the practical side. In short: it is nice to have a series of research results and charts, but what does it sound like? What are the effects of too aggressive denoising? What is the effect of tweaking a parameter one way or the other?

What I ended up doing was a very simple real-time demo of spectral subtraction.

It ticked the boxes:

  • It worked on a standard Windows laptop (the year was 2001).
  • It had a nice, simple UI with controls to adjust the parameters of the denoising algorithm.
  • The live playback made it easy to reproduce typical denoising side effects, such as musical noise.
  • It had some positive impact beyond the time of the project. I vaguely remember John mentioning it was demoed alongside a poster presentation at a conference. The demo was also used for a few years in the lab to illustrate some of the work done there, and it ended up as a nice opener for discussions with collaborators and research sponsors.

What did I get from this project?#

First, a good knowledge of spectral subtraction, which I ended up… not using much in other speech processing tasks 1. Then:

  • My name (3rd author) on my first ever research publication (doi:10.1109/ICDSP.2002.1028255).
  • Some interesting coding experience: “a Windows C++ application built with Visual C++ 6.0, using MFC for the UI and the Win32 multimedia API (winmm) for real-time audio capture and playback”.
  • A textbook optimisation problem: sorting, and finding the quantile of a 1-in 1-out sliding window (sliding-median.md).

How it works#

To remove noise, you need to know what it looks like. And to know what it looks like, you need to work in the frequency domain: is it the low hum of a fan, a high-pitched siren, or neither, with the energy spread evenly across all frequencies (we call that white noise)?

So we cut the signal into many short slices (32 ms each, a new one every 16 ms) and look at the spectrum of each slice:

// one 32 ms slice, windowed, then FFT
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
  re[i] = x[start + i] * win[i];
  im[i] = 0;
}
fft.forward(re, im);
for (let k = 0; k < bins; k++) power[k] = re[k] * re[k] + im[k] * im[k]; // |X_k|^2

Then, frequency by frequency, we look back over the last 0.5 s, that is 31 slices. The loud slices, with high energy, are probably speech; the quiet ones are probably just noise. The median, the middle value of the 31, gives a guess of the noise level that speech does not pull up, as long as speech is there less than half of the time:

history.push(power); // the last 0.5 s of slices, kept sorted in each bin
history.query(0.5, noise); // N_k: the median (quantile 0.5) in each bin

We might not pick the value right in the middle, though: a lower quantile (below 0.5) gives a quieter, more cautious noise estimate, while a higher one (above 0.5) gives a louder estimate, with the risk of picking up speech as noise. The web app has a Quantile slider to try it.

Now that we have a guess of what the noise is like, we just have to subtract it.

But how much should we subtract? Let’s have a parameter, α, for this. And what if we subtract too much? Let’s have a second parameter, β, to keep some of the original signal:

const p = power[k];
const clean = Math.max(p - alpha * noise[k], beta * p); // |Y_k|^2

Finally, have we made everything too loud or too quiet? We do not rebuild the sound from scratch: each frequency of the original slice is turned down by a gain, and the slices are put back together. The gain is never above 1, so nothing gets louder, and β sets how far down a frequency can go (with β = 0.05, about 13 dB quieter at most):

const g = p > 0 ? Math.sqrt(clean / p) : 0; // G_k
re[k] *= g; // G_k X_k: only the magnitude changes, the noisy phase is kept
im[k] *= g;

The slices overlap by half and each one fades in and out, so when they are added back together the joins are seamless. The whole loop is in specsub.ts.
